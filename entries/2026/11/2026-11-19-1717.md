You've probably heard of this pop science thing with the eyes and the brain that travels back in time because saccades and all.
It did attack my youtube suggestions more than once.

If you have no clue what I'm talking about, the eye does some movements when you're (figuratively) not looking.
It does that because it only has a small area it can focus on, but you do want a full, somewhat high-res picture. So essentially it's stitching together a panorama.

This, then, through some handwaving, is connected to phenomena like you looking at a ticking clock and the time for the second hand to move feeling significantly longer than 1 second.

Now, the pop science take on that is that "the brain goes back in time and inserts data it only could've had later - chronologically."
The framing then usually goes, "Wow! This breaks our understanding of reality, meaning that we have so much still to learn."

However, given that it's an extraordinary claim, one might argue that a more fitting framing would be "Wow! This breaks our understanding of reality. That probably means it's bullshit."

The reason I am telling you about this is that there is this other theory that explains the same data but doesn't rely on breaking the linearity of time.

Specifically, viewing sensory processing as a prediction engine, with the real data being processed on higher levels not being the actual raw feed, but the error/delta to the expected data.
This, paired with the theory that the mind has no fixed clock, but rather a somewhat dynamic tick rate - with our perception of time being based on how many ticks we saw.

So you look at the watch. You get a new scene.<br/>
The dynamic tick rate rises, because we have a large delta since something else is in frame. Possibly also partially as a hardwired response to the movement itself? Or any other data sources indicating "processing needed."

The added compute recalibrates the model. The error goes down. The tick rate goes down again.<br/>
This gives you a first second that feels significantly longer, but the brain doesn't need to move back in time, nor does it have to buffer the feed shown to your "self" for as long as would be necessary to allow for such editing.

What it might be doing instead is keeping the output of the predictor running with as much known valid data as is available. Plus, of course we probably have some amount of latency in the system. But that's about it. This might work not because the brain constructs an actual valid sequence of events, but because it loves continuity.
So the predictor might've given you something somewhat resembling a second hand, and once it actually appears, the probabilities snap to that, and desire for continuity makes it to be having always been that.<br/>
Which is _not_ time travel or editing, but the impossibility to accurately check back on a prior system state once new data was already ingested. The moment you consciously think about the state of that watch, the promise has already been resolved, basically, and ambiguities have already collapsed.


So, my assumption would be that running that predictor with that level of ambiguity is an inherently high-energy-need state, meaning higher tick rate, meaning time dilation that gets un-dilated the moment the visual feed stabilizes and actual truth appears.
